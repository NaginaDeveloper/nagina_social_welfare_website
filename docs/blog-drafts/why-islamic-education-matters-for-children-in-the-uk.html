For a Muslim child growing up in the UK, school fills most of the day. Islamic education has to find its place around it, and it is worth getting right. The first word revealed in the Holy Qur'an was "Read" (Surah al-Alaq, 96:1), and the Holy Qur'an asks, "Are those who know equal to those who do not know?" (Surah az-Zumar, 39:9). Knowledge is at the heart of the faith. This article looks at why a Madrasa education matters for children in the UK and how parents can support it.

More than reading the Holy Qur'an

A good Madrasa teaches children to recite the Holy Qur'an properly, with tajweed, but it does not stop there. At our Madrasa in Peterborough, children also learn Hadith, the Seerah (the blessed life of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ), Fiqh (everyday rulings), Aqeedah (belief) and good manners. Together these give a child a reliable foundation: what they believe, why they pray, and how to behave towards family, neighbours and classmates.

Knowing who you are

Children in the UK move between school, friends, social media and home. A regular place where they learn about their faith, with teachers they know and classmates who share it, helps them feel settled in their identity. They can answer questions from friends with confidence, and they have a community to turn to.

Character, not only information

Good Islamic education shapes character: honesty, patience, respect for parents and teachers, kindness to those in need. Children learn these best by seeing them practised. That is why the example of teachers, and of parents at home, matters as much as the lesson itself.

Supporting your child at home

A Madrasa works best when home supports it. Some simple habits make a real difference:

  • Keep a routine. A fixed time for homework from the Madrasa, even ten minutes, builds consistency.
  • Revise little and often. A few minutes of daily recitation is better than one long session each week.
  • Talk about what they learned. Ask your child to explain a lesson or a dua to you.
  • Pray together. Children copy what they see, and a family that prays together makes faith feel natural.
  • Stay in touch with the teachers. Raise concerns early and celebrate progress.

Choosing a Madrasa: what to ask

When you look for a Madrasa, it is reasonable to ask:

  • What is taught, and in what order?
  • What are the class times, and how are children grouped by age?
  • How does the Madrasa keep children safe? A good Madrasa has a clear safeguarding policy, and you should be able to read it.
  • How do parents and teachers communicate?
  • How are fees handled and how are families supported?
